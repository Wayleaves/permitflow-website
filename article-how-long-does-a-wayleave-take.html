How Long Does a Wayleave Application Take in South Africa?

Timelines vary significantly by authority. Here's a realistic breakdown — authority by authority — and what you can do to avoid the delays that are actually avoidable.

Back to all articles

The honest answer is: it depends on the authority, the complexity of the crossing, and the quality of your application. A well-prepared municipal wayleave in a cooperative metro can be approved in four weeks. An Eskom transmission line crossing can take five months. And if your application is incomplete and gets returned, the clock resets.

Here are realistic timelines based on our experience across hundreds of applications in all nine provinces.

Timeline by Authority

Municipal (local) 4–12 weeks 3 weeks 6+ months

The critical path principle: On any project crossing multiple authorities, your completion date is set by your slowest approval — not your average. If you have municipal approvals in hand but Eskom is still processing, you're waiting. Always identify your longest-lead authority first and submit to them immediately.

What Affects the Timeline?

These are the factors that most consistently extend or compress approval times:

  • Application completeness. This is the biggest variable you control. An incomplete application gets returned — and the clock resets from zero on resubmission. At SANRAL, a returned application can add 6–8 weeks to your timeline instantly.
  • Complexity of the crossing. A simple perpendicular underground crossing is straightforward to review. A longitudinal installation running 500m along a road reserve, or a crossing at a major interchange, takes much longer.
  • Capacity at the authority. Some municipalities and provincial departments are under-resourced. Applications sit in queues. This is where consistent, professional follow-up makes a practical difference.
  • Time of year. Government capacity drops significantly in December and January. Applications submitted in November often don't move meaningfully until February. If your project has a hard deadline, factor this in.
  • Landowner objections. Under the new 2026 ICASA framework, ECNS licensees are now required to consult with landowners before deployment. A landowner objection triggers a dispute resolution process that can add months.

What the New 2026 Regulations Add to the Timeline

For ECNS licensees (fibre and telecoms operators), the Draft ICASA Rapid Deployment Regulations introduce mandatory prior approvals and landowner consultations before deployment begins. The cumulative effect of obtaining all required prior approvals — across multiple authorities — could push effective approval periods beyond 90 working days for complex projects.

This isn't a reason to panic, but it is a reason to start earlier. Projects that previously budgeted 6–8 weeks for permitting may now need 12–16 weeks or more if they involve multiple authority crossings and any private landowner engagement.

How to Keep Your Application Moving

  • Submit a complete, correct package first time. Every returned application adds weeks. The most reliable way to shorten timelines is to never need a resubmission.
  • Submit to all authorities simultaneously. Don't wait for municipal approval before applying to SANRAL. Run all applications in parallel from day one.
  • Follow up consistently. Most authorities will not proactively notify you of progress. Regular, professional follow-up keeps your application visible and moving.
  • Know your contacts. A direct relationship with the right person in each authority — not just the general inbox — is worth weeks of processing time.
  • Identify your critical path early. Know which authority is likely to be your slowest and escalate proactively when that application stalls.
