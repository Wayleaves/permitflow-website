Municipal vs SANRAL vs Eskom Wayleave — What's the Difference and Who Do You Apply To?

One of the most common points of confusion in infrastructure permitting is working out which authority to approach. The answer depends entirely on whose land or infrastructure corridor you're crossing — and getting it wrong wastes weeks.

Back to all articles

South Africa has no single wayleave authority. Unlike many countries where infrastructure permitting is centralised, here the relevant authority depends entirely on who owns or manages the land or corridor your infrastructure needs to cross. A 10km fibre route through a mixed urban environment could require simultaneous applications to SANRAL, a metropolitan municipality, a local municipality, and Eskom — each with different requirements, different formats, and different timelines.

Understanding the landscape before you start is not optional. Submitting to the wrong authority, or missing one entirely, can hold up an approved project for months while the rest of your permissions wait.

The Quick Decision Framework

Before diving into each authority in detail, here's the fast version. Ask these questions in order:

  1. Is the crossing on a national road (N-route)? → SANRAL
  2. Is the crossing on a provincial road (R-route) outside a municipality? → Provincial Department of Transport or Public Works
  3. Is the crossing on a road within a municipal area? → The relevant municipality
  4. Does the route run under or adjacent to an Eskom power line servitude? → Eskom Transmission or Distribution (depending on voltage)
  5. Does the route cross a Transnet railway reserve? → Transnet
  6. Does the route cross private land outside any of the above? → Negotiate directly with the landowner

In practice, most infrastructure routes cross several of these simultaneously — which is why multi-authority project management is the norm, not the exception.

SANRAL — National Roads

South African National Roads Agency (SANRAL)

Jurisdiction: All national roads — the N-route network, both tolled and untolled.

What triggers a SANRAL application: Any infrastructure crossing, running within, or installed adjacent to a national road reserve. This includes underground crossings, aerial crossings, longitudinal installations within the reserve, and any works that may affect the structural integrity or drainage of the road.

Key requirement: Engineering drawings stamped by a registered engineer, GPS coordinates, method statement, traffic accommodation plan, and a detailed motivation letter.

Typical timeline6–16 weeks
Regional offices4 (Northern, Southern, Eastern, Western)
Technical difficultyHigh

SANRAL's review process is technically rigorous. Their engineers examine every submission carefully, and incomplete or poorly prepared applications are returned without processing. The clock resets on resubmission. Of all the wayleave authorities in South Africa, SANRAL is the one where professional preparation pays for itself most clearly.

One important point: SANRAL manages four regional offices, and your application must go to the correct region. An application submitted to the wrong regional office will be transferred internally — adding unnecessary weeks to your timeline.

Provincial Authorities — Provincial Roads

Provincial Departments of Transport / Public Works

Jurisdiction: Provincial roads — R-routes outside municipal boundaries. Each of the nine provinces has its own department managing its provincial road network.

What triggers an application: Any infrastructure crossing or installation within a provincial road reserve. This includes rural R-routes connecting towns and districts, as well as some R-routes that pass through urban areas (though in urban areas, roads are often under municipal jurisdiction).

Key requirement: Each province has its own application format and requirements. There is no national standard for provincial road wayleaves — requirements vary meaningfully between, say, Limpopo and the Western Cape.

Typical timeline4–12 weeks
Authorities9 provincial departments
Technical difficultyMedium (varies by province)

Provincial road wayleaves are often underestimated. Rural fibre routes in particular can cross dozens of provincial roads, each requiring its own application to the provincial department. In provinces with stretched capacity — which includes most of them — follow-up and relationship management is essential to keeping applications moving.

Municipalities — Municipal Roads and Public Land

Metropolitan and Local Municipalities

Jurisdiction: All roads, pavements, parks, and public open space within municipal boundaries. South Africa has 8 metropolitan municipalities and 226 local municipalities — each manages its own wayleave process.

What triggers an application: Any infrastructure crossing or running within a municipal road reserve, pavement, or public open space. This covers the vast majority of urban fibre and telecoms deployments.

Key requirement: Varies significantly by municipality. Larger metros (City of Joburg, City of Cape Town, eThekwini) have formal online portals and detailed submission requirements. Smaller local municipalities may have informal processes that require relationship management and direct engagement.

Typical timeline4–12 weeks
Authorities234 municipalities
Technical difficultyLow to high (varies widely)

Municipal wayleaves are the most variable part of the landscape. The City of Tshwane and City of Cape Town have well-developed, relatively predictable processes. But many smaller municipalities have no formal wayleave procedure at all, and applications end up being processed informally through the relevant technical department — which requires knowing who to contact and how to follow up.

The most common mistake: Treating all municipalities as equivalent. A submission format that works perfectly for the City of Johannesburg will often be rejected outright by a smaller local municipality that has its own requirements — and vice versa.

Eskom — Power Line Servitudes and Substations

Eskom (Transmission and Distribution)

Jurisdiction: Eskom's registered servitude areas around its power lines, substations, and electrical infrastructure. Eskom has two relevant divisions: Transmission (high voltage, 66kV and above) and Distribution (lower voltage, serving end users).

What triggers an application: Any infrastructure crossing under, over, or alongside an Eskom power line servitude. This is common for fibre routes running through industrial areas, along highway corridors, or through agricultural land where Eskom lines are prevalent.

Key requirement: Eskom has strict electromagnetic compatibility and safety requirements. Applications require engineering drawings showing clearances from the power line, compliance with Eskom's technical standards, and often a formal crossing agreement rather than a simple wayleave letter.

Typical timeline8–20 weeks
DivisionsTransmission and Distribution
Technical difficultyHigh

Eskom wayleaves are consistently the slowest in the South African landscape. Eskom's internal review process involves multiple technical sign-offs and does not move quickly regardless of pressure. Building 3–5 months into project timelines for any crossing involving an Eskom high-voltage servitude is prudent planning, not pessimism.

The distinction between Eskom Transmission and Distribution matters practically. A crossing under a 132kV transmission line goes through a different internal process than a crossing adjacent to a 11kV distribution line. Submitting to the wrong division adds significant delay.

Transnet — Railway Reserves

Transnet Freight Rail

Jurisdiction: Transnet's railway reserves — the land corridor on either side of South Africa's rail network managed by Transnet Freight Rail.

What triggers an application: Any infrastructure crossing a railway reserve or running within it. Rail crossings are technically demanding because of the vibration environment, the drainage requirements, and the strict safety clearances required from the live rail infrastructure.

Key requirement: Detailed engineering drawings with specific attention to depth of burial, casing requirements, and anti-vibration measures. Transnet has its own technical standards that must be followed precisely.

Typical timeline10–20 weeks
Technical difficultyVery high

Private Landowners

Where infrastructure needs to cross private land that falls outside any of the above authorities' jurisdiction, you're dealing directly with the landowner. There is no formal application process — instead, you negotiate an access agreement or wayleave licence directly.

Under the new ICASA Rapid Deployment Regulations, ECNS licensees are now required to conduct formal consultations with private landowners and conclude written access agreements before deployment begins. This formalises what was previously often an informal arrangement — and makes professional support for the negotiation and agreement drafting more important.

What About Overlapping Jurisdictions?

This is where it gets genuinely complicated. Some road reserves sit at jurisdictional boundaries — an R-route that enters a municipal area may be managed by either the province or the municipality depending on the specific arrangement in that area. Some Eskom servitudes run parallel to road reserves managed by SANRAL or a municipality — meaning you may need approvals from both.

How to handle overlapping jurisdictions: When in doubt, apply to both. The cost of an unnecessary application is a small fee and some administrative effort. The cost of missing a required authority and having to restart after the fact is measured in months. In cases where jurisdiction is genuinely unclear, our team can research the correct authority before you commit to an application.

Managing Multiple Applications Simultaneously

For any linear infrastructure project of meaningful length — a fibre route of more than a few kilometres, a pipeline running through mixed terrain — you will almost always be dealing with multiple authorities simultaneously. The practical challenges are:

  • Each authority has different requirements — there's no single application package that works everywhere
  • Each authority has different timelines — the critical path through your project is the slowest authority, not the average
  • Each authority requires its own follow-up and relationship management — applications don't move themselves
  • Approvals from different authorities may arrive at different times, creating sequencing challenges on the ground

This is the core operational challenge of wayleave management — and the reason why projects that try to manage it entirely in-house often find that permitting becomes the unexpected bottleneck in an otherwise well-planned rollout.
