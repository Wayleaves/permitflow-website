SANRAL Wayleave Requirements — What to Submit and Why Applications Get Rejected

SANRAL wayleave applications have strict technical and administrative requirements. Here's exactly what documents you need, what SANRAL's reviewers look for, and the most common reasons applications are rejected or delayed.

Back to all articles

SANRAL — the South African National Roads Agency — is the authority responsible for the national road network. Any infrastructure that needs to cross, run alongside, or be installed within a national road reserve requires a SANRAL wayleave. This includes fibre and telecoms cables, pipelines, power lines, water mains, and any other utility crossing an N-route.

SANRAL wayleaves are among the more technically demanding applications in South Africa's permitting landscape. SANRAL's engineering teams review submissions carefully and will reject or return incomplete applications without hesitation. Understanding exactly what they require — before you submit — is the difference between a smooth approval and a months-long delay.

Which Roads Require a SANRAL Wayleave?

SANRAL manages South Africa's national road network — the N-routes. These are numbered N1 through to N17 (and beyond in some classifications) and include both tolled and non-tolled national roads.

If your infrastructure crosses or runs within the road reserve of any national road, you need a SANRAL wayleave. This applies regardless of:

  • Whether the road is tolled or untolled
  • Whether the crossing is above or below ground
  • The duration of the installation (permanent or temporary)
  • The size or type of infrastructure involved

Not sure if the road is a SANRAL road? The easiest check is the road number. N-routes are SANRAL. R-routes are provincial. Roads within urban areas without a route number are typically municipal. If you're uncertain, our team can confirm the relevant authority before you invest time in preparing an application.

The Core Document Requirements

SANRAL requires a comprehensive application package. Missing even one element will typically result in your application being returned. Here is what must be included:

1. Formal Application Letter

A formal letter addressed to the relevant SANRAL Regional Office (not the head office — SANRAL operates through four regional offices: Northern, Southern, Eastern, and Western). The letter must identify the applicant, describe the infrastructure, state the location, and request permission for the specific works.

2. Engineering Drawings

This is where most applications fall short. SANRAL requires professionally prepared engineering drawings that show:

  • A locality plan placing the crossing in its wider geographic context
  • A layout plan showing the road reserve boundary, the carriageway, and the proposed infrastructure route
  • Cross-section drawings showing the depth of burial, the casing or sleeve specifications, and the clearance from the road surface
  • Longitudinal section where relevant
  • Details of any thrust bores or directional drills (if the installation is trenchless)

Drawings must be signed and stamped by a registered professional engineer or technologist. Freehand sketches, Google Maps screenshots, or unmarked aerial images will not be accepted.

3. GPS Coordinates

The exact location of the crossing must be provided in decimal degrees format. SANRAL's reviewers cross-reference these against their own GIS records. Approximate or incorrect coordinates are a common rejection trigger.

4. Motivation Letter or Technical Report

A written motivation explaining why the infrastructure is needed, what public benefit it serves, and why the chosen crossing point was selected. For fibre and telecoms applications, this typically references the operator's ECNS licence and the role of the infrastructure in the national broadband rollout.

5. Method Statement

A construction methodology describing how the installation will be carried out — including how traffic will be managed during construction, what trenchless techniques will be used for the crossing itself, and how the road reserve will be reinstated after installation.

6. Traffic Accommodation Plan

For any works that may affect traffic flow on or adjacent to the national road, a formal Traffic Accommodation Plan (TAP) is required. This must comply with the South African Road Traffic Signs Manual (SARTSM) Volume 3 and must be prepared by someone competent to do so.

7. Proof of Professional Registration

SANRAL requires proof that the engineer who prepared the drawings is registered with ECSA (Engineering Council of South Africa).

8. Company Documentation

CIPC company registration certificate, proof of VAT registration (where applicable), and identification of the responsible person signing the application.

SANRAL's Regional Offices — Submit to the Right One

SANRAL applications must be submitted to the correct regional office. Submitting to the wrong region is a surprisingly common mistake that adds weeks of delay while the application is transferred internally.

  • Northern Region — Limpopo, Mpumalanga, North West, and parts of Gauteng
  • Southern Region — Western Cape, Eastern Cape, and Northern Cape
  • Eastern Region — KwaZulu-Natal and parts of the Eastern Cape
  • Western Region — Gauteng, Free State, and parts of North West

Each regional office has its own submission email, its own contact persons, and — importantly — its own processing quirks. Building a relationship with the right contacts in each region is one of the practical advantages an experienced wayleave consultant brings.

How Long Does SANRAL Take?

For a complete, correctly prepared application, SANRAL's internal target is 30 working days. In practice, straightforward applications in less congested regions are often processed in 6–8 weeks. Complex crossings — particularly those involving major interchanges, high-volume roads, or technically demanding installations — routinely take 12–16 weeks or longer.

Incomplete applications are returned without being processed, and the clock effectively resets when you resubmit. This is why first-submission quality matters so much.

The Most Common Rejection and Delay Reasons

Based on our experience across hundreds of SANRAL applications, these are the issues that most frequently cause rejections or delays:

Most common rejection triggers:

  • Engineering drawings not stamped by a registered engineer. SANRAL will not accept drawings prepared by unregistered persons, regardless of their technical quality.
  • Incorrect or missing GPS coordinates. Even a transposed digit can cause a return — SANRAL's GIS team verifies every submission against their road reserve boundaries.
  • No method statement or an inadequate one. Stating "the cable will be installed by directional drilling" is not a method statement. SANRAL wants detail: equipment, dimensions, staging areas, reinstatement specifications.
  • Submitted to the wrong regional office. This adds time — sometimes weeks — while the application finds its way to the right desk.
  • Missing traffic accommodation plan. Any works with a potential traffic impact require a TAP. Submitting without one, hoping it won't be flagged, rarely works.
  • Cross-section drawings missing or inadequate. SANRAL's engineers need to see the exact depth of burial and the clearance from the road layer works. Plans without sections will be returned.
  • Application letter addressed incorrectly or too vague. The letter must name the specific road, the chainage or GPS location, the infrastructure type, and the works method. Vague letters are returned for more information.

What Happens After Approval?

Once SANRAL approves your wayleave, the approval letter will specify the conditions of the permission. These typically include:

  • The approved construction method
  • Reinstatement requirements and standards
  • Notice requirements before commencing works
  • SANRAL's right to inspect during and after installation
  • Compliance with the approved traffic accommodation plan
  • The term of the permission (usually with renewal provisions)

It is critical to read the approval conditions carefully and comply with them. SANRAL has the right to require removal of any infrastructure installed in breach of permit conditions — at the operator's cost.

Do the New ICASA Regulations Affect SANRAL Applications?

For ECNS licensees, yes — indirectly. The new ICASA Rapid Deployment Regulations require prior approval from all relevant authorities before deployment begins. A SANRAL wayleave forms part of that prior approval chain. The practical effect is that ECNS licensees deploying in or adjacent to national road reserves need to factor SANRAL's approval timeline into the overall regulatory approval sequence — not treat it as a parallel track.
